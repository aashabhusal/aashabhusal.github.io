Final-year BBS Research

Comparative Analysis of Capital Structure:
Privately-Owned vs Government-Owned Banks

Nabil Bank (private) vs Rastriya Banijya Bank (government)
Five fiscal years: FY 2077/78 – 2081/82 (July 2020 – July 2025)
Shanker Dev Campus, Tribhuvan University · Supervised by Prof. Durga Datt Pathak

Overview

This study compares how ownership shapes the way two Nepali commercial banks manage their capital. Over five years of audited data, it measures leverage, capital adequacy, profitability, and lending behaviour side by side. The headline finding: ownership drives capital-management style. The private bank prioritises efficiency and shareholder returns within regulatory limits, while the government bank prioritises stability, reserves, and its public mandate — an acceptable trade-off for its role, but one that leaves capital working less hard.

Objectives

  • Compare the capital structure and leverage of a private vs a government bank.
  • Measure how efficiently each bank converts its equity base into profit.
  • Assess how ownership influences lending strategy and risk appetite.
  • Test whether headline figures such as EPS give a fair picture of performance.

Methodology

The analysis rests on quantitative ratio analysis of published financial statements, cross-read four ways:

  • Ratio analysis — debt-to-equity (D/E), capital adequacy ratio (CAR), return on equity (ROE), return on assets (ROA), earnings per share (EPS), and credit-to-deposit (CD) ratio.
  • Comparative analysis — the two banks placed side by side on every metric.
  • Trend analysis — year-over-year movement across the five fiscal years.
  • Descriptive statistics — mean, standard deviation, and coefficient of variation to gauge consistency, plus a simple correlation between leverage and profitability.

Results were presented in structured tables and charts, with the underlying figures compiled in a worksheet appendix.

Data Sources & Scope

The study uses secondary quantitative data only, drawn from:

  • Audited annual reports of both banks.
  • Quarterly financial statements and Basel III disclosures.
  • Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) directives and publications.
  • The banks' official websites and financial data portals (used for cross-verification).

Figures were extracted item by item from published statements, entered into structured worksheets, and then computed. Time span: five fiscal years, FY 2077/78 to FY 2081/82 (July 2020 – July 2025).

Key Findings

Metric Nabil (private) Rastriya Banijya Bank (government)
Leverage (D/E)~7.5–8×~6.2×
Capital adequacy (CAR)~12% (near regulatory floor)12–14% (thicker cushion)
Return on equity (ROE)~11%~6%
Return on assets (ROA)~1.17%~0.84%
Credit-to-deposit (CD)~84% (aggressive)60–70% (conservative)
Earnings per share (EPS)~Rs. 22–27~Rs. 22–27 (similar)

1. Leverage & capital structure

Nabil ran higher leverage (~7.5–8×) on a thinner capital buffer, with CAR sitting near the regulatory floor around 12%. Rastriya Banijya Bank carried lower leverage (~6.2×) and a thicker cushion of 12–14%, built largely from retained reserves.

2. Profitability gap

On a similar equity base, Nabil earned roughly double the return: ROE of ~11% against RBB's ~6%, and ROA of ~1.17% versus ~0.84%. The private bank extracted far more profit from equivalent capital.

3. Lending strategy

Nabil lent aggressively, with a credit-to-deposit ratio around 84%. RBB stayed conservative at 60–70%, consistent with its public-service role and lower risk appetite.

4. Why EPS is misleading

Both banks reported similar earnings per share (~Rs. 22–27), which makes them look comparable at a glance. But that similarity masks Nabil's roughly 2× higher capital efficiency — RBB's smaller share base offsets its lower total profit. The gap only surfaces when profitability ratios are read alongside leverage, not from EPS alone.

Conclusion

Ownership drives capital-management style. The private bank (Nabil) prioritises efficiency and shareholder returns, operating with high leverage inside regulatory constraints. The government bank (RBB) prioritises stability, reserves, and public mandates over maximum profitability — an acceptable trade-off given its role, but one that results in lower capital efficiency.

Recommendations

  • Rastriya Banijya Bank — improve capital utilisation through prudent, measured lending expansion, so its large reserve base works harder.
  • Nabil Bank — build reserves to strengthen a thin capital buffer, given its aggressive leverage.

Limitations

The study relied entirely on published secondary sources — no primary data or internal bank documents. A few quarterly or unaudited figures were used to fill gaps and were flagged for reconciliation against final audited reports; items marked "see AR" indicate figures pending final verification.
